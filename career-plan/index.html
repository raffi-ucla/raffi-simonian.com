Steps to a Successful Career

The framework I use in career counseling, made interactive. It starts with Ikigai — a Japanese concept meaning “a reason for being,” the sweet spot where passion, mission, vocation, and profession align. Reflect on the four questions below, then let AI turn your answers into personalized career recommendations.

💡 Ikigai is a framework for finding purpose and fulfillment. The four questions map your passions, strengths, the world's needs, and the market — your target roles and industries live where the answers overlap. The more specific your answers, the more personalized the recommendations.

Step 1 · Self-Awareness: discover your reason for being

Self-awareness precedes job titles, salaries, or degrees. The more detail you give, the better the recommendations.

1. What do you love?

What activities or hobbies bring you happiness in your life?

💭 Prompts
  • What activities make you feel most alive and fulfilled?
  • What are the things you could spend hours doing without feeling drained?
  • When do you feel a sense of joy, excitement, or deep satisfaction?

2. What are you good at?

What skills or talents come naturally to you?

💭 Prompts
  • What are your strengths?
  • What do you excel at?
  • What skills or abilities come naturally to you?

3. What does the world need?

What societal challenges, causes, or needs resonate with you and inspire a desire to contribute positively to the world?

💭 Prompts
  • What problems or issues in your community or globally do you feel passionate about solving?
  • How can you contribute to making a positive impact on society or the lives of others?
  • What skills or expertise do you possess that could address a societal or global need?

4. What can you be paid for?

What skills, services, or expertise can you offer that others find valuable enough to compensate you for?

💭 Prompts
  • What skills, services, or products can you offer that people are willing to pay for?
  • How can you leverage your abilities to create a source of income?

Step 2 · Career Exploration: discover your options

There are over 900 careers in 14 industry clusters — most people never see the full menu. Start with a free snapshot: the themes where your answers overlap and three directions worth exploring. When you book a coaching session (Step 5 below), your included prep intake turns these answers into a full career project plan — your first year month by month, plus a 3-year roadmap — that we review together, live.

Step 3 · Career Planning: set a direction

Career planning does not require a perfect or permanent decision. Set an initial direction that aligns your natural abilities with the realities of the labor market — projected wages, demand, and where your skills can develop enough to excel.

  1. Choose a career direction. From your Personalized Career Assessment, pick 1–2 target roles that fit your strengths, interests, and labor-market realities (pay, demand, growth).
  2. Identify required skills. For each role, list the academic, technical, and employability skills (communication, teamwork, leadership) employers expect.
  3. Map your gaps. Compare those lists to your current skills and highlight what to close in the next 1–3 years.

Step 4 · Skills-Based Education: build the plan and work it

More than an education plan — a roadmap based on skills and competencies. Education is no longer a single track: degrees, industry certifications, work-based learning, entrepreneurship, and employability skills all work together.

Skills→Competencies→Credentials→Achievement

The pathway behind every step on this page: individual skills compound into demonstrable competencies, competencies earn credentials the market recognizes, and credentials unlock achievement — the role, income, and impact you set out for. Every learning choice below should move you one step along this chain.

What the market says about human skills

91%of employers rate human skills as equal to or more important than technical ability (LinkedIn)
73%of talent-acquisition leaders rank critical thinking the #1 skill they recruit for — AI skills rank 5th (Korn Ferry)
71%of global leaders say the ability to function amid constant change matters more than it did a year ago (Harvard Business Publishing)
  1. Design a skills-based learning path. For each gap, pick a route: university courses or minors, certificates and bootcamps, internships and research, volunteering and leadership roles, online courses and self-study projects.
  2. Map out a networking plan. Tools like Connect The Dots (maps your network from your email conversations) and Nira (conversational search over your LinkedIn network) keep your relationships working for you.
  3. Get ready for interviews. Use the PAR method on your resume to show impact with metrics, and practice behavioral interviews with LinkedIn’s guide to the questions interviewers ask.
  4. Follow creative job-search tactics. See how to get a job anywhere with no connections and how one job-seeker beat 2,000+ applicants.
  5. Build a simple timeline. Decide what you will do this quarter, this year, and next year — then review and adjust each term, keeping the plan focused on transferable skills, not checking boxes.

Part 1 · Know yourself

We are shaped constantly by social media, family expectations, and stories about success. These questions cut through that noise — there are no right or wrong answers, only honest ones.

1. Whose career do you admire?

Picture a specific person — a family member, a professor, someone from your network or online. Whose path comes to mind when you imagine your dream job, and what draws you to it?

2. What makes you lose track of time?

Moments of real engagement — the activities that feel both effortless and energizing to you.

✓ Pre-filled from Step 1 — “What do you love?”. Edit here to refine it.

3. What are your natural strengths?

Not just resume skills — the things people compliment you on, or that feel easy to you but hard for others.

✓ Pre-filled from Step 1 — “What are you good at?”. Edit here to refine it.

4. What drains your energy?

Knowing what depletes you matters as much as knowing what fuels you. Which tasks, environments, or dynamics leave you exhausted?

5. What do you want to be known for in 5–10 years?

When colleagues or friends describe you professionally, what do you want them to say? This reveals your deeper aspirations.

Part 2 · Define your non-negotiables

Every job involves tradeoffs. Naming your non-negotiables helps you filter opportunities and avoid regret later.

6. What are your must-haves in a role?

Check everything that genuinely matters to you, then rank your top 3 below — and for each, note the tradeoff you would accept to get it.

7. What are your deal-breakers?

What would make you turn down an offer regardless of salary or prestige? Toxic culture, values misalignment, unsustainable hours, no growth…

Part 3 · Align with your values

Your career will take a large share of your time and energy. Does the work you are considering align with what matters to you beyond a paycheck?

8. What issues or causes do you care deeply about?

Think beyond work: environmental sustainability, education access, healthcare, economic opportunity, creative expression, social justice — whatever matters to you in the world.

✓ Pre-filled from Step 1 — “What does the world need?”. Edit here to refine it.

9. How important is it that your work directly serves those values?

1 means you are happy to contribute in your personal life instead; 5 means your work itself must align. Then say why you chose that rating.

Part 4 · Future-proof your skills

The market evolves quickly. Technical skills matter, and adaptable skills carry you through every transition.

10a. Which technical skills do you want to develop?

AI, data analysis, coding, design, finance — the hard skills you want your next role to grow.

10b. Which transferable skills do you want to develop?

Communication, problem-solving, project management, strategic thinking — the skills that travel with you.

11. Which skills do you already have to leverage?

What you already bring from coursework, internships, projects, jobs, or extracurriculars — recognize it before you build more.

✓ Pre-filled from Step 1 — “What can you be paid for?”. Edit here to refine it.

Part 5 · Find your proximal peers

The right role models are slightly ahead of you — close enough that their path feels usable, not unattainable.

12. Who is 2–3 years ahead of you on a path you admire?

List 2–3 people whose careers inspire you but feel within reach. What specific decisions or moves of theirs could you learn from?

Part 6 · Look ahead (but stay grounded)

Your first role does not define your career — but it sets a trajectory.

13. Where do you see yourself in 3–5 years?

Not necessarily a job title — the type of work, the impact you want to have, the expertise you want to build.

14. What realistic milestones would get you there?

Break the vision into achievable steps — what would Year 1, Year 2, and Year 3 each need to deliver?

Part 7 · Your materials

Your plan is built around what you can already show — and I use these in my personal review.

15. Your resume or CV

Upload your resume (PDF, Word .docx, or .txt) — I receive the file personally for my review, and its text is extracted below to power your plan. No public link needed. Prefer to type? Paste the text straight into the box.

16. Link your resume, portfolio, or LinkedIn

Optional: a link to your portfolio or LinkedIn profile — I open these personally during my review. (No need to link your resume if you uploaded it above.)

Part 8 · Planning inputs

The two answers that turn reflection into a schedule.

17. Your timeline

When do you need the next role, and what does achievement look like by then? For example: employed in the field within 9 months; promoted within 2 years; business launched by 2028.

18. Constraints & resources

What the plan must work around and with: location or relocation limits, hours per week you can invest, budget for courses, family commitments, visa or licensing requirements.
